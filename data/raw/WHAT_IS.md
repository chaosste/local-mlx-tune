| What is 'epistemic injustice'? | Epistemic injustice occurs when someone's knowledge or capacity as a knower is unfairly dismissed or ignored, often due to prejudice. In plain English, it is the harm done when society refuses to believe you or cannot understand you because of who you are. Advanced terminology divides this into testimonial injustice (when a speaker is misjudged as less credible due to bias) and hermeneutical injustice (when a listener cannot understand a speaker because society lacks the shared concepts to grasp their marginalized experience).Example: When the medical establishment dismisses Indigenous healing practices as mere "folk wisdom" rather than empirical truth, elevating Western science as the only valid standard. In everyday life, it manifests when a doctor dismisses a woman's reports of pain because of gender biases. |
| What is 'the shadow' (and golden shadow)? | The shadow refers to the hidden, repressed, and often guilt-laden parts of the personality that we are ashamed of and keep hidden from society. Originating in depth psychology, it contains our "uncivilized desires and emotions that are incompatible with social standards". However, this psychological basement is not entirely negative. These rejected, lowly parts of ourselves often contain our unlived potential (conceptually known as the golden shadow), making them the very "keys to growth" and essential for genuine self-knowledge.Example: A society avoiding its collective shadow might manifest toxic behaviors, such as modern epidemics of covert depression or violence among men who suppress their emotional truth. On a personal level, someone who harshly judges others for being "lazy" might be projecting their own repressed, shadow-desire to rest. |
| What is 'cognitive liberty'? | Cognitive liberty is the fundamental human right to think independently, use the full spectrum of your mind, and maintain absolute autonomy over your own brain chemistry. It merges freedom of thought with bodily autonomy, asserting that the individual—not corporate or government interests—has sole jurisdiction over their own mental processes. It includes both the right to use tools to alter or enhance consciousness and the right to refuse coercive technologies.Example: The legal and ethical battle over drug decriminalization is a cognitive liberty issue. In the workplace, if an employer mandates the use of attention-enhancing "smart drugs" to increase productivity, it would be a severe violation of an employee's cognitive liberty. |
| What is a 'biosemiotic stance'? | A biosemiotic stance is an ecological framework that views all living organisms as active interpreters of their world, rather than just biological machines. It recognizes that meaning-making exists everywhere in nature. It requires distinguishing between inert matter that simply reacts to forces (pleroma) and living beings that actively evaluate and interpret differences as meaningful information (creatura). It acknowledges that every organism has a unique, species-specific sensory world (Umwelt) through which it understands reality.Example: Recognizing that a dog sniffing out cancer or trees communicating through fungal networks are engaging in valid, complex meaning-making. Instead of asking "what is it like to be a toaster?", this stance respects the profound difference between a manufactured object and a living, interpreting badger. |
| What is 'amelioration'? | Amelioration is a "band-aid" intervention that improves an individual's immediate suffering without fixing the broken, unjust systems that caused the suffering in the first place. It is often contrasted with transformation, which aims to dismantle toxic social conditions so that interventions are no longer needed. While giving individuals tools to survive is better than doing nothing, stopping at amelioration misattributes systemic failures as personal deficits.Example: Prescribing anti-anxiety medication or cognitive enhancers to a student struggling in an underfunded, overcrowded school. It helps the student survive the day, but it completely ignores the structural problem of the inadequate education system. |
| What is 'rhizomatic accountability'? | Rhizomatic accountability is a non-hierarchical, continuous, and deeply collaborative way of building relationships and sharing power with marginalized communities. Instead of a top-down, one-off extraction of data (like a standard institutional survey), it treats community expertise as dynamic, plural, and constantly shifting. It seeks to cultivate highly localized, grassroots knowledge ecologies that directly counter the mass-scaling, extractivist tendencies of capitalism.Example: A healthcare organization wanting to understand community needs doesn't just hire a consultant to run a focus group. Instead, they partner long-term with local activists, sharing decision-making power, and perhaps gathering insights while collectively tending a community garden or making art. |
| What is 'dark reflexivity'? | Dark reflexivity is the deliberate practice of stepping back, letting go of the need for absolute certainty, and becoming comfortable with the unknown. It asks us to recognize the limits of our own understanding and cede control, while still maintaining responsibility. It acts as an antidote to ego-inflation and "saviorism," requiring us to repeatedly ask how our own liberation is bound up with the struggles of others.Example: A therapist or researcher realizing they cannot "fix" a marginalized community from an expert distance. Instead, they admit what they do not know, sit with the discomfort of mystery, and allow themselves to "feel their way together" with the people they are trying to help. |
  

| What are 'psychedelic assemblages'? | An assemblage is a network of ideas, people, technologies, and practices that come together around a specific concept. A psychedelic assemblage refers to the different ways society packages and understands psychedelic substances. The sources identify three main types: the entheogenic (spiritual/Indigenous use focused on community and nature), the medical (clinical focus on treating brain chemistry), and the capitalist (corporate focus on patents, profit, and productivity).<br><br>Example: The stark contrast between taking psilocybin mushrooms in an Indigenous ceremony to connect with ancestors (entheogenic), being prescribed a synthetic psilocybin pill in a sterile clinic for depression (medical), and a tech CEO microdosing LSD to be more productive at a start-up (capitalist). |

| What is 'edgework'? | Edgework refers to voluntary, high-risk activities that push individuals to their absolute physical, psychological, or functional limits. By courting extreme danger or altered states—such as through extreme sports, intense rituals, or the use of psychoactive drugs—humans attempt to transcend their mundane reality and temporarily experience a sense of superhuman or "divine" existence.<br><br>Example: A wingsuit flyer jumping off a mountain or a free-diver plunging to extreme depths on a single breath. They are engaging in edgework to break free from the safety of modern society, seeking a transcendent, almost spiritual state of extreme presence and ecstasy. |
  

| In what ways is the psychedelic experience a suitable metaphor for psychological decolonization? | A: The psychological processes initiated by psychedelics closely mirror the internal dismantling and reconstruction required for decolonizing the mind. First, psychedelics provoke "ego dissolution," which challenges the Euro-American concept of a unified, fixed, and highly individualistic self that is upheld by the coloniality of power. Second, they induce mental plasticity, which disrupts entrenched thought patterns and aids in the "self-unlearning" of Westernized ideological conditioning. Third, they cultivate a profound sense of relationality and interconnectedness, shifting values away from self-centered goals and toward an ecological consciousness that bridges the colonial dualisms of mind/matter and human/nature. Ultimately, psychedelics can act as subversive "technologies of the self" that deterritorialize consciousness and allow individuals to escape dominant, paralyzing perspectives. |

**What are the risks of "colonial extractivism" within the modern psychedelic renaissance?**  
The contemporary boom in psychedelic interest risks reproducing the very imperial practices of historical colonialism. The market profits heavily off substances sourced from plants and fungi that have a deep history of Indigenous use, leading to issues of biopiracy and cultural appropriation. Furthermore, Western psychedelic science operates under a "white-dominant framework" that frequently excludes Indigenous people and people of color from research. By arbitrarily applying Western medical or psychological categories to Indigenous worldviews without cultural competence, the psychedelic renaissance risks committing epistemological violence and "epistemicide".  
**Q: How does the Western emphasis on individualism conflict with decolonial psychedelic praxis?**  
**A:** Western frameworks tend to view psychedelic use as a hyper-individualistic practice of introspection and self-guided healing, which starkly contrasts with the communal practices of Indigenous sacraments. This individualistic focus risks reinforcing "solipsistic withdrawal," where individuals retreat from the community into private healing. A genuinely decolonial approach requires collaborative knowledge-building and collective action to address structural social inequities, rather than simply optimizing an individual to feel better within an unjust system.  
**Q: Can psychedelics be used to successfully decolonize hegemonic masculinity?**  
**A:** Psychedelics have the potential to disturb hegemonic masculine structures—which are deeply tied to colonial ideals of individualism, rationalism, and detachment—by shifting men toward values of empathy, altruism, and interconnectedness. However, this outcome is not guaranteed and is fraught with paradoxes. Because psychedelics amplify the "set and setting," they possess a "political pluripotency" and can easily be co-opted to reinforce existing hierarchies. In fact, psychedelics have historically been used to foster warrior ethics, and today they are sometimes used as vehicles for right-wing white identity politics or neoliberal productivity (repressive desublimation). Therefore, decolonizing masculinity through psychedelics requires rigorous self-reflection, community accountability, and an active resistance to capitalist and patriarchal co-optation.
